The Rise and Fall… and Rise of the Mullet

An old silhouette, glam-rock fame, and a second act shaped by more than one scene.

By Satanica Lux · The Vice City Sinner · November 2026

I have a confession. Every time a fresh mullet scrolls past on my feed, I stop. I can’t help it. Some haircuts are just haircuts, and some have a whole biography. The mullet has had one hell of a public life: glam-rock fame, a very public fall, and a second act nobody saw coming.

Older Than You Think

The mullet’s basic shape is much older than the name. Nearly 3,000 years ago, the ancient Greek poet Homer described warriors who wore their hair long at the back. Centuries later, the writer Plutarch explained that they kept the front short so enemies couldn’t grab it in a fight. It wasn’t called a mullet, but the outline sounds familiar: business in the front, party in the back.

Glam Rock Gets a Haircut

In the early 1970s, Suzi Fussey cut David Bowie’s hair for the Ziggy Stardust character he played onstage. Bowie had shown her a photo of a model from designer Kansai Yamamoto’s first London show: short, spiky, bright red, and longer at the neck. Fussey dyed the cut red and got it to stand up. You do not need to know the whole Ziggy story to get why the look mattered. It turned an old outline into a deliberate piece of stage design: sharp around the face, dramatic from the side, impossible to mistake for ordinary hair.

The shape also helped Bowie play with the line between masculine and feminine styling. In a decade when pop stars were building complete visual identities, the mullet could be both a haircut and a character choice.

The Golden Age of Hockey Hair

By the 1980s the mullet was everywhere. Rock stars, movie heroes, your cousin. Patrick Swayze wore one to throw drunks out of a Missouri bar in Road House. Andre Agassi wore one on the tennis court, and in his 2009 memoir, Open, he admitted a good chunk of it was a hairpiece. He spent the 1990 French Open final, his first Grand Slam final, praying it wouldn’t fall off onto the clay. He lost. Image is everything.

It was never only a dude’s haircut, either. Some lesbians also used the mullet as a public signal of identity in the eighties. Joan Jett’s spiky black cut sits somewhere between shag and mullet. Hockey made it practically part of the uniform. Czech rookie Jaromír Jágr arrived in Pittsburgh in 1990 and went on to become one of the NHL’s biggest stars; the long hair became part of his signature look. You do not have to know his stats to recognize the mullet.

Then came 1992, when Billy Ray Cyrus released “Achy Breaky Heart.” His label promoted the song with a dance of its own, helping feed the line-dancing craze. The song was huge, and so was the hair.

The cut even came with its own motto, the one people still say with a grin: business in the front, party in the back. It might be the most efficient life philosophy ever written. Nobody has pinned down who said it first, but everyone seems to know it.

A Name, a Song, and a Punchline

For most of its heyday, the mullet didn’t have a name. It was just a haircut. That changed in 1994, when the Beastie Boys released “Mullet Head,” a song that takes shots at Van Damme, Kenny G in a Trans Am and “names like Billy Ray,” and gives the barber his instructions: number one on the side, don’t touch the back. The Oxford English Dictionary lists it as the earliest known use of “mullet” for the hairstyle and says the band “apparently coined, and certainly popularized” it. In 1995 the Beasties’ own magazine, Grand Royal, ran a six-page feature called “Mulling Over the Mullet,” complete with alternative names like “Hockey Player Haircut” and “Soccer Rocker.”

Once it had a name, it became an easy punchline. By the late nineties and early 2000s, the mullet was comedy shorthand for somebody stuck in another decade. David Spade’s 2001 Joe Dirt gave that joke a face.

I laughed too. But the mullet got a raw fucking deal. It never stopped being fun. We just stopped being brave.

The Alt-Scene Detour

Queer and punk scenes had kept the mullet in circulation long before the latest mainstream wave. It could be a DIY statement, a playful refusal of gendered hair rules, or simply a cut someone liked. Today the silhouette has more range than the hard-edged 1980s version: shaggy layers, curly texture, soft fringe, cropped sides, and wolf-cut cousins all blur the line. There is no single modern mullet or single meaning. Around 2010, alt communities were also experimenting with blunt fringes, chopped layers, asymmetrical cuts, undercuts, and bright color. Some crossed into mullet territory; plenty did not. Hair was being used as design, not just grooming.

Television kept the mullet visible, too. Danny McBride wore an especially memorable one as Kenny Powers in Eastbound & Down, a loud, vain, chaotic former baseball player who looked like he had never met a mirror he could not challenge. The joke depended on the haircut being instantly legible. The joke also kept the silhouette in circulation.

The Comeback Had More Than One Beginning

Billie Eilish had an accidental brush with the revival in November 2019, although she told TMZ a dye job had burned half her hair off and “that shit is not on purpose.”

A high-profile moment came in January 2020, when Miley Cyrus posted a new cut by Sally Hershberger: a feathery shag, choppy fringe in the front, long in the back. Mullet revivals were already appearing in fashion and alternative scenes, so this was not a clean starting gun. It did make an irresistible headline: Billy Ray’s daughter embracing the cut her father made famous. Hershberger told Vogue she thought of David Bowie, then said, “This isn’t her father’s mullet — this is glam rock.”

Then the rest of 2020 happened. Salons closed, many people spent more time at home, and plenty tried cutting their own hair in the bathroom mirror. Shaggy, imperfect styles suddenly made sense. Tiger King hit Netflix in March, putting Joe Exotic’s mullet on screens while much of the country was stuck at home. Social media, celebrity looks, runway styling, and DIY hair all helped the style travel; no single event explains the whole revival. By September, i-D had declared 2020 the year of the mullet, with TikTok doing a lot of the work. Rihanna sent models down the Savage x Fenty runway in mullets that fall, then wore one herself in Barbados. That December, Troye Sivan showed up with a curly one in his “Easy” video with Kacey Musgraves.

Then came the wolf cut, a soft, layered cousin generally traced to South Korean salons. It blurred the line between shag and mullet even more. Hockey never fully let go, either. Jágr cut his mullet for a while, then grew it back at 43 while playing for the Florida Panthers, after goalie Roberto Luongo asked him to. The Panthers posted regular hair updates. South Florida has always had its priorities straight.

Plenty of people got their 2020 mullet as a joke. A lot of them kept it anyway. I like what this second act has done: it let the cut move across scenes. Boys, girls, everybody in between: it can work on all of us, and nobody has to audition for it.

So, Should You Get One?

The right haircut is less about your face shape than your nerve. Get one if you like a little drama, the kind that makes people look twice, not the messy kind. Go to somebody good, because a mullet lives or dies by its layers and this is not the moment for a bargain cut. Stay open to the variations, from the shag and the wolf cut to the soft mullet and the full classic. Be patient, because growing out the back takes time and the awkward phase is part of the story. And accept that somebody is going to say “business in the front.” Let them have their moment.

The mullet survived glam rock, country radio, and twenty years of jokes, and it is still standing with the party fully intact. Not many of us can say that.
