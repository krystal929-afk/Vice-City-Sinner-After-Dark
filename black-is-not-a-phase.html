Black Is Not a Phase

Why do we treat black clothes as a problem with an expiration date?

By Satanica Lux · The Vice City Sinner · November 2026

When somebody wears a lot of black, the question tends to arrive disguised as reassurance: “You won’t dress like that forever.” A phase has an end date. The person saying it is betting that you will return to a version of normal they recognize.

Black has carried more meanings than mourning for a long time. In Europe, it could signal wealth and status when rich, lasting black dye was expensive. It was also used for formal and religious dress. By the nineteenth century, black had become the prescribed color of mourning, with elaborate rules about how long a person should wear it and what shades or fabrics were appropriate. Black did not belong only to grief, but grief made it the meaning people most readily recognize.

By the early twentieth century, black was also a modern fashion choice. Coco Chanel’s simple black dress helped make the color a symbol of style and ease, not just ceremony. A black suit could mean business or formality; a little black dress could move from afternoon to evening. The social meaning changed with the garment, the occasion, and who was wearing it. There was no single day when black stopped being funeral clothes. People kept adding meanings to it.

Johnny Cash made that choice explicit. In “Man in Black,” he connected his dark clothes to poor and beaten-down people, prisoners, the sick, and the hungry. The black suit was not a costume for mourning a private loss; it was a public reminder of people he thought the country ignored. You can disagree with the song’s politics and still understand what the outfit was doing: turning a personal uniform into a statement.

That is part of the appeal of dressing in black. It gives the eye fewer colors to sort through, so shape and texture start doing more work. Leather catches light differently from velvet. A sheer sleeve changes the silhouette; a row of studs or a silver chain interrupts it. A band shirt, a sharp suit, a patched vest, a long skirt, a hoodie, and a fitted dress can all be black and say completely different things. The color does not flatten the choices. It makes the choices easier to see.

Different scenes have used that visual language in their own ways. Punk can make black look thrown together on purpose: torn fabric, safety pins, slogans, paint. Goth can build with lace, velvet, Victorian references, and romantic drama. Industrial style might use utility shapes, boots, and hardware. Glam can make black glossy, tight, and theatrical. None of those looks tells you exactly what the person wearing it believes. Clothes can signal a scene, but they are not a full biography.

And sometimes black is just useful. It is easy to combine, easy to repeat, forgiving after a long shift, and available in almost every kind of clothing. In South Florida, wearing black in July may earn a practical question about heat before anyone asks if you are in a phase.

Calling a look a phase lets people dismiss it without asking what it means to you. If you change your style, they say they were right. If you keep it, they can say you never grew up. Either way, the judgment is ready before you are. The phrase says more about someone else’s expectation that you become conventional than about the clothes.

Maybe one day black gives way to bright pink. Maybe it does not. A look can be temporary without being fake; a lasting style can change without becoming a failure. The point is not to prove anyone wrong by dressing the same at forty-five as you did at fifteen. Nobody else gets to decide when you have outgrown yourself.

Wear black because you want to. Leave it when you don’t. It was yours either way.
